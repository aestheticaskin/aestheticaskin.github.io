How Chemical Peels Work — A Complete Guide for Indian Skin

You've heard the name. Maybe even Googled it at 11pm after another month of stubborn pigmentation. But what actually happens when acid meets skin — and is it safe for you?

Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg

MD Dermatology · Aesthetica Skin Clinic, Punawale, Pune

She had tried everything. Vitamin C serums. Niacinamide. That expensive Korean sunscreen her colleague swore by. But every monsoon season, the dark patches crept back — across her cheeks, along her jawline, stubborn as old furniture stains.

She was 34, a software engineer from Hinjewadi, and she sat across from me in the consultation room with a printout of ingredient lists and a look that said: I've done my research. Now what?

I told her what I tell every patient who walks through that door with years of pigmentation and a drawer full of skincare disappointments: "The products were never going to fix this. They can't reach where the problem lives."

That is the story of chemical peels. And if you've been battling pigmentation, acne marks, or dull skin — it might be your story too.

The Problem With the Surface

Your skin is a city with layers. At the very top — the stratum corneum — live the old, dead cells that are days away from shedding anyway. Most over-the-counter skincare products never get past this outer borough. They moisturise the surface, add a temporary glow, maybe deliver a little antioxidant activity. But the real problems — melanin deposits from acne, hyperpigmentation from years of Pune's UV exposure, the shallow craters of healed breakouts — they live deeper. In the epidermis. Sometimes in the upper dermis.

That is where a chemical peel goes.

"Think of a chemical peel as a controlled reset — we're asking the skin to shed what isn't serving it anymore and replace it with something healthier beneath."

— Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg, MD Dermatology

What Actually Happens When Acid Meets Skin

A chemical peel applies an acid solution — glycolic, salicylic, lactic, TCA, or a blend — to the skin surface. The acid disrupts the chemical bonds between cells in the outer skin layers, causing controlled exfoliation. As these layers shed over the following days, they take with them the damage they contain: the dark melanin, the rough texture, the congested pores.

But here's what makes it more than just aggressive exfoliation: the controlled wounding triggers your skin's healing cascade. New cells rise. Collagen synthesis is stimulated. The replacement skin is fresher, smoother, more evenly pigmented. You're not just removing the old — you're prompting the body to build something better in its place.

The depth of this process depends entirely on which acid is used, at what concentration, and for how long it's left in contact with the skin. Which brings us to the question I hear most often in clinic.

Which Peel is Right for You?

There are three broad categories of chemical peel — superficial, medium-depth, and deep — and they are not interchangeable. Choosing the wrong one for your skin tone or concern is one of the fastest ways to make Indian skin worse, not better.

Superficial Peels

Glycolic, Lactic, Salicylic, Mandelic Acid

These work on the outermost layers of the epidermis. They are the workhorses of maintenance — excellent for routine brightening, clearing congestion, managing active acne (especially salicylic), and giving dull skin its glow back. Downtime is minimal — mild redness for 24–48 hours at most. For most Indian patients, this is where we start.

Medium-Depth Peels

TCA (Trichloroacetic Acid), Jessner's Solution

These penetrate into the mid-epidermis and upper dermis — reaching the deeper pigmentation that superficial peels cannot touch. They cause visible peeling and shedding over 5–7 days and yield significantly more dramatic results for melasma, post-acne marks, and mild scarring. These require proper pre-treatment preparation for Indian skin to prevent rebound pigmentation.

Combination Peels

Custom Blended Protocols

At Aesthetica, we frequently create combination protocols — layering acids strategically to address multiple concerns in a single session while managing the risk for Indian skin tones. These are tailored at each visit based on how your skin has responded, not applied from a standard recipe.

The Indian Skin Complication Nobody Talks About Enough

Here is the uncomfortable truth about chemical peels and Indian skin: done incorrectly, they can make pigmentation dramatically worse.

Fitzpatrick skin types III–V — which describes most Indian skin — have melanocytes that are easily provoked. Any inflammation, including the controlled inflammation of a peel, can trigger a surge of melanin production called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). This is not rare. I have seen patients come to me after salon peels that left them darker than when they started.

The solution is not to avoid peels. It is to do them properly.

  • A dermatologist should assess your Fitzpatrick type and choose the peel accordingly
  • Pre-treatment priming with tyrosinase inhibitors (kojic acid, arbutin) for 4–6 weeks reduces PIH risk significantly
  • Starting with lower concentrations and building up prevents overwhelming the skin
  • Strict sun protection during and after treatment is non-negotiable in Pune's climate
  • Post-peel prescription topicals accelerate results and reduce risk of rebound

A note from Dr. Prachi: The most common mistake I see is patients getting aggressive TCA peels without adequate skin preparation. For Indian skin, the preparation phase — the 4–6 weeks before the peel — is just as important as the peel itself. Don't skip it.

What to Realistically Expect

Let's be honest about the timeline, because unrealistic expectations are the enemy of good outcomes.

After your first superficial peel, you will likely see a glow — brighter, cleaner-looking skin — within 5–7 days as the shedding completes. This is encouraging. It is not the final result. Significant improvement in pigmentation or texture requires a series: typically 4–6 sessions spaced 2–4 weeks apart, with maintenance sessions thereafter.

Deeper peels produce more dramatic change per session — but come with more downtime and more risk on Indian skin. The trade-off is real. Most of my patients in Punawale have professional lives and cannot afford a week of visible peeling skin. We choose the approach that fits their life, not just the one that sounds most impressive in theory.

The Patient I Mentioned at the Beginning

She started with a series of mandelic acid peels — gentler than glycolic on Indian skin, with lower PIH risk. We primed her skin for six weeks first. We added a prescription kojic acid and niacinamide topical. We were meticulous about sunscreen.

By session four, she sent a photograph from Goa — no filter, taken in direct afternoon light — with a message that said: "I've stopped carrying concealer."

That is what a well-chosen, properly executed chemical peel does. Not the miracle stories. The quiet, consistent, compounding change that one morning you realise has transformed how you move through the world.

Common Questions About Chemical Peels

What does a chemical peel actually do to the skin?

It applies an acid solution that dissolves the bonds between damaged skin cells, causing controlled shedding. This triggers the skin's healing response — accelerating cell turnover and revealing fresher, more evenly toned skin, while stimulating collagen production in deeper layers.

Are chemical peels safe for Indian skin?

Yes — when selected and performed by an experienced dermatologist. Indian skin requires careful acid selection, proper pre-treatment preparation, and diligent sun protection. Without these, the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) is significant. With them, chemical peels are among the most effective treatments available for Indian skin concerns.

How long before I see results?

Initial brightening is visible within 5–7 days of your first peel. Significant improvement in pigmentation, texture, or scarring requires 4–6 sessions over 2–3 months. Results build progressively — the skin improves with each session as the underlying damage is addressed layer by layer.

Which peel is best for pigmentation on Indian skin?

For superficial pigmentation and post-acne marks, mandelic or glycolic acid peels are safer starting points for Indian skin. For deeper melasma or stubborn marks, TCA peels combined with prescription topicals yield stronger results — but require more careful management. The right choice depends on a clinical assessment of your pigmentation type and depth.

Scalp Care at Home — What Dermatologists Actually Recommend

Most of what the internet tells you about scalp care is either harmless myth or quietly damaging. Here's what actually works — and what to stop doing immediately.

Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg

MD Dermatology · Aesthetica Skin Clinic, Punawale, Pune

She had been oiling her hair every night since she was twelve. Her mother had done it. Her grandmother had done it. There was an entire mythology built around the ritual — hot oil, circular massage, sleeping with a towel on the pillow. It was, she had always been told, the secret to thick Indian hair.

She was 28 when she came to see me. Her scalp was inflamed, chronically itchy, and scattered with seborrheic dermatitis patches that had been there, with varying intensity, for almost three years. Her hair was thinning at the temples. The overnight oil — the very practice she believed was protecting her hair — had been quietly feeding a fungal imbalance on her scalp for years.

She wasn't doing anything wrong by the logic she had been given. But the logic was incomplete.

This is the problem with scalp care advice in India. It is passed down through generations with tremendous affection and authority — but it was developed without any understanding of the scalp's microbiome, its sebum regulation, or the fungal organisms that thrive when conditions are right. Much of it is harmless. Some of it causes real damage. And almost none of it distinguishes between what's good for the hair shaft and what's good for the scalp.

These are not the same thing.

"Hair is dead protein. The scalp is living skin. What you do at the roots determines everything — but most people treat their hair and completely ignore the ecosystem it grows from."

— Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg, MD Dermatology

The Myths Worth Dismantling First

Myth

"Oil your scalp overnight — it feeds the follicles."

Fact

Hair follicles are nourished by blood supply — not topical oil. Leaving oil on the scalp for extended periods feeds Malassezia fungus (which drives dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis), clogs follicle openings, and creates a film that requires aggressive shampooing to remove — which itself damages the scalp barrier. Oil the lengths of your hair if you wish, but rinse it out within 2–3 hours.

Myth

"Washing hair every day causes hair loss."

Fact

Washing frequency does not cause hair loss. The hairs you see in the drain after washing were already in the telogen shedding phase — shampooing only detaches them. What causes scalp damage is the wrong shampoo, water temperature, or technique — not the act of washing. In Pune's climate, an oily scalp benefits from washing every 1–2 days.

Myth

"Natural ingredients are always safe on the scalp."

Fact

Lemon juice (highly acidic, disrupts pH), raw egg (potential Salmonella, biotin-blocking avidin), onion juice (can cause contact dermatitis), fenugreek paste (documented cases of allergic scalp reactions) — all popular home remedies, all potentially harmful. "Natural" and "safe" are not synonyms. The scalp's pH is 4.5–5.5. Anything significantly outside that range disrupts its microbiome.

Myth

"Dandruff is caused by a dry scalp — moisturise it."

Fact

In most cases, dandruff is driven by Malassezia — a yeast that thrives in sebum-rich environments. Adding moisture or oil typically worsens dandruff, not improves it. True dry scalp (flaking without oiliness) is far less common and requires a different approach entirely. Misidentifying the cause leads to months of worsening symptoms.

What Your Scalp Actually Needs — The Clinical Routine

Think of the scalp as facial skin with a specific microclimate — one that is warmer, oilier, and more prone to fungal activity due to the hair canopy trapping heat and humidity. The principles of good scalp care mirror the principles of good skincare: cleanse appropriately, don't strip or suffocate, maintain the right pH, and treat conditions specifically rather than generically.

wash

Choose the right shampoo — and rotate it

For oily or dandruff-prone scalps: zinc pyrithione, ketoconazole 2%, or piroctone olamine. For sensitive or normal scalps: gentle, sulphate-free formulas at pH 4.5–5.5. Rotate between two shampoos every 4–6 weeks to prevent the scalp microbiome from adapting and reducing effectiveness.

thermostat

Wash with lukewarm water — never hot

Hot water strips the scalp's sebum layer aggressively, triggering compensatory overproduction of oil within hours. It also vasodilates scalp blood vessels in a way that temporarily increases inflammation. Lukewarm water cleanses effectively without disrupting the scalp's sebum balance.

touch_app

Massage with fingertips — not nails

The physical act of scalp massage — even during shampooing — stimulates blood flow to the follicles. Use the pads of your fingertips in gentle circular motions. Scratching with nails creates micro-abrasions that invite secondary infection and worsen an already inflamed scalp.

air

Dry gently and thoroughly

A damp scalp left for hours creates a humid environment that fungal organisms thrive in. Gently squeeze (don't rub) with a microfibre towel and allow to air dry fully before tying or braiding hair. If using a hairdryer, keep it on cool or low heat and maintain 15cm distance from the scalp.

block

Give the scalp time to breathe between styles

Tight ponytails, braids, and buns maintain constant tension on the follicle — contributing to traction alopecia over time. Heavy extensions and wigs prevent the scalp from breathing, trapping sweat and creating a warm, humid microenvironment. Alternate your styles and give your scalp at least two days a week completely free of tension styling.

The Pune-Specific Scalp Problem Nobody Talks About

Pune's water is hard — high in calcium and magnesium. When hard water evaporates from hair and scalp after washing, it leaves behind mineral deposits that build up over time, creating a film that blocks follicle openings, makes hair feel rough and dull, and causes scalp irritation that most people attribute to dandruff or dry scalp.

If you have noticed that your hair feels different after washing at someone's home in Mumbai or on holiday — softer, more manageable — the water is very likely a factor. Two solutions:

  • A shower head filter — inexpensive, effective, and replaces cartridges every 3–4 months
  • A chelating shampoo once a month — specifically formulated to break down mineral deposits that regular shampoo cannot remove

What At-Home Care Can and Cannot Do

A good at-home scalp routine is not a treatment for hair loss. It is a maintenance protocol — one that preserves the scalp environment between clinical sessions, prevents conditions from worsening, and gives clinical treatments the best possible environment to work in.

If you are experiencing active, progressive hair thinning, significant dandruff that does not respond to medicated shampoo within 6 weeks, scalp itching and inflammation, or visible scalp patches — these are clinical problems that require a clinical assessment. A trichoscopy at Aesthetica takes 20 minutes and provides a precise diagnosis. What you do at home matters enormously. But it cannot replace understanding what is actually happening beneath the surface.

The most useful thing you can do tonight: Look at your scalp under good light. Is it producing more oil than usual? Flaking? Itching at specific spots? Tender to touch? These observations — brought to a dermatologist — are the beginning of an accurate diagnosis. Your scalp is telling you something. The question is whether you know how to listen.

She Changed One Thing

The woman who had been oiling her scalp every night since she was twelve made one change: she stopped. No drama, no complicated new routine — just stopped leaving oil on her scalp overnight.

Within three months, the seborrheic dermatitis had reduced by roughly 70%. The itching was almost gone. We added a ketoconazole shampoo twice a week and a gentle scalp serum between washes. The inflammation cleared. The thinning at her temples stabilised.

She still oils her hair sometimes. Just the lengths, just the ends, just for a few hours before washing. The ritual her grandmother taught her isn't entirely wrong — it just needed to stay off the scalp.

Small knowledge. Significant difference.

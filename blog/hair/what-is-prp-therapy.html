What is PRP Hair Therapy? A Complete Patient Guide

It sounds almost implausible — take a vial of your own blood, spin it, inject it back into your scalp, and watch dormant hair follicles wake up. But the science is real, the results are documented, and for the right patient, it changes everything.

Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg

MD Dermatology · Aesthetica Skin Clinic, Punawale, Pune

He had started noticing it at 31. Not dramatically — not the way hair loss happens in film montages, where someone looks in a mirror one morning and gasps. It was quieter than that. The shower drain, clogged a little more often. A photograph from a friend's wedding where the light caught the crown of his head at an angle that he didn't recognise as himself. His fingers running through his hair in the morning and coming away with more than they used to.

By the time he sat across from me at the clinic, he had tried three shampoos, a biotin supplement from a pharmacy in Baner, and a minoxidil foam that he'd stopped using after three months because he couldn't see a difference.

"I read about PRP online," he said. "But I can't tell if it's real medicine or just expensive nonsense."

It was the most honest question a patient had asked me in weeks. And it deserved an honest answer — not a brochure.

Let's Start With What Hair Loss Actually Is

Before PRP can make sense, the biology has to make sense. Because PRP is not a treatment for hair loss in general. It is a treatment for a very specific thing happening inside your scalp.

Each hair on your head grows from a follicle — a tiny, living structure embedded in the scalp. Follicles cycle through three phases: anagen (active growth, lasting 2–6 years), catagen (a brief transition), and telogen (resting, before the hair sheds and the cycle restarts).

In androgenetic alopecia — the most common form of hair loss, affecting both men and women — a hormone called DHT (dihydrotestosterone) binds to receptors in genetically susceptible follicles and progressively miniaturises them. The anagen phase shortens. Hairs grow finer, shorter, and lighter with each cycle. Eventually, the follicle produces only a thin, colourless vellus hair — and then, if the process continues long enough, nothing at all.

The follicle is not dead at this point. It is dormant. And dormant follicles can still be woken up — if the right biological signals reach them in time.

That is precisely what PRP does.

"PRP does not create hair from nothing. It wakes up follicles that have gone quiet but haven't yet closed permanently. The window matters — which is why timing the treatment correctly is everything."

— Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg, MD Dermatology

What PRP Actually Is — The Science Without the Jargon

PRP stands for Platelet-Rich Plasma. Your blood is made of red blood cells, white blood cells, plasma — and platelets. Platelets are the tiny cells responsible for clotting when you're injured. But they carry something else: a concentrated payload of growth factors — proteins that signal healing, cell proliferation, and tissue regeneration.

In a PRP session, a small amount of your blood — typically 15–30ml — is drawn and placed in a centrifuge. The centrifuge spins at high speed, separating the blood into its components by density. The platelet-rich layer — now concentrated at 3–5 times its normal blood concentration — is carefully extracted.

This concentrated PRP is then injected into the scalp in a systematic grid pattern across the areas of thinning. The growth factors — PDGF, VEGF, TGF-beta, IGF-1 among others — diffuse into the surrounding tissue and reach the hair follicles directly.

The result? The follicle's stem cells are activated. Blood supply to the follicle improves. The anagen growth phase is extended. Miniaturised follicles begin to grow fuller, stronger hair again.

What Happens in a PRP Session at Aesthetica

1

Trichoscopy Assessment

Before your first PRP session, Dr. Prachi performs a trichoscopy — a high-magnification scalp analysis — to confirm follicle viability and map the areas most likely to respond. This step distinguishes patients who will benefit from PRP from those who won't.

2

Blood Draw

A small vial of blood is drawn from your arm — similar to a routine blood test. Takes under two minutes. The draw is processed immediately in an on-site centrifuge.

3

Centrifugation

The blood spins at precisely calibrated speed for 8–10 minutes, separating into layers. The platelet-rich layer is carefully extracted into a sterile syringe — your PRP dose, ready for injection.

4

Scalp Numbing

A topical numbing cream is applied to the scalp for 20–30 minutes. Most patients are surprised by how comfortable the injections are — a mild pressure rather than anything sharp.

5

PRP Injections

The PRP is injected in a systematic grid across the target areas using fine needles. The entire injection phase takes 15–20 minutes. Most patients feel mild pressure and occasional brief sensitivity — not significant pain.

Who It Works For — And Who It Doesn't

This is the conversation I have with every patient, because online searches rarely give a straight answer here.

PRP works for patients whose follicles are still alive but underperforming. That means:

  • Early to moderate androgenetic alopecia — the follicle is miniaturised but still present
  • Diffuse hair thinning — generalised reduction in hair density across the scalp
  • Alopecia areata — patchy loss where the immune system has suppressed follicle activity
  • Post-partum hair loss — where telogen effluvium has disrupted the growth cycle
  • Hair fall related to stress, illness, or nutritional deficiency — once the underlying cause is addressed

PRP does not work for patients with advanced baldness where large areas of scalp are smooth and the follicles have been permanently lost. A trichoscopy examination is essential for determining which category you fall into — and at Aesthetica, we perform this assessment before committing any patient to a PRP course.

The honest timeline: Most patients notice reduced hair fall by session 2. Visible new growth — thin, baby hairs in previously bare areas — typically appears from session 3–4. Significant density improvement is visible at session 5–6. Anyone promising dramatic results in one session is not telling you the truth.

The Man Who Asked the Right Question

His trichoscopy confirmed what I suspected: Norwood III androgenetic alopecia — the crown and frontal hairline thinning, but the follicles were still viable. He was in the window.

We started a course of six PRP sessions, four weeks apart. I combined the third and fourth sessions with scalp microneedling to amplify growth factor penetration into the follicle layer — a combination that consistently produces better results than PRP alone in my clinical experience.

By session four, his wife noticed before he did. She pointed to the crown of his head one morning and said, "Is that new hair?" He came in for his fifth session and held up his phone with a photograph taken that week at the same angle as the wedding photo he'd been carrying around in his head for two years. The difference was visible. Not a full head of hair — that was never the promise. But a meaningful, real, undeniable recovery.

"Is it real medicine or expensive nonsense?" he had asked me.

I showed him his own before-and-after trichoscopy images side by side. The follicle density map was measurably denser. The miniaturised hairs had thickened.

"Real medicine," he said. "Expensive, but real."

That is the honest answer. PRP hair therapy — done properly, with the right diagnostic foundation, on the right patient, at the right stage of hair loss — is among the most effective non-surgical interventions available. It will not regrow hair that is permanently gone. But for the millions of people in whom the follicles are simply sleeping? It can wake them up.

How many PRP sessions are needed?

A standard initial course is 4–6 sessions spaced 4 weeks apart. Most patients notice reduced shedding by session 2 and visible new growth from session 3–4. Maintenance sessions every 3–6 months sustain results long-term.

Is PRP hair therapy safe?

Extremely safe — PRP uses your own blood, eliminating any risk of allergic reaction or rejection. Minor soreness and redness at injection sites resolves within 24–48 hours. All sessions at Aesthetica are performed using sterile, single-use equipment under clinical supervision.

How long do PRP results last?

The initial course typically delivers results lasting 12–18 months. Without maintenance, hair fall gradually returns as the growth factor stimulus fades. Regular maintenance sessions every 3–6 months significantly extend and sustain the improvement.

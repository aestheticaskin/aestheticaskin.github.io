7 Common Causes of Hair Loss in Women — and What to Do

Female hair loss is one of the most emotionally heavy experiences a woman can carry — and one of the most consistently misdiagnosed. The cause is almost never just "stress." Here's what is actually happening.

Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg

MD Dermatology · Aesthetica Skin Clinic, Punawale, Pune

She was 33 and she walked into the consultation room carrying a Ziploc bag. Inside it were the hairs she had collected from her pillow, her hairbrush, and the bathroom floor over the course of a single week. She laid it on the desk between us without saying a word.

She didn't need to. That bag said everything — the months of watching it happen, the mounting anxiety, the evenings spent down internet rabbit holes that all ended at the same useless answer: probably stress.

"Three different doctors told me it's stress," she said. "But my life isn't more stressful than it was two years ago. Why is this happening now?"

It was a brilliant question. Because "stress" is both the most common explanation for female hair loss and one of the laziest. Yes, stress can trigger hair shedding — but it rarely acts alone, and it is almost never the root cause. In over a decade of treating female hair loss in Pune, I have seen the same causes appear again and again — and stress is not usually at the top of the list.

Here are the seven causes I see most often. And crucially — what to do about each.

"Female hair loss is almost always treatable — but only if you identify the actual cause. Treating the wrong thing wastes months. A proper diagnosis changes everything."

— Dr. Prachi Gupta Garg, MD Dermatology

The 7 Most Common Causes

01

Iron Deficiency

Nutritional · Very Common in India

Iron deficiency is the single most common nutritional cause of female hair loss in India — and the most frequently missed. Ferritin (stored iron) is the critical marker, not just haemoglobin. Many women with serum ferritin below 70 ng/ml experience significant diffuse hair thinning even when their haemoglobin is technically within normal range.

Heavy periods, vegetarian or vegan diets, repeated pregnancies, or poor dietary iron absorption all deplete ferritin over time. Hair follicles are highly metabolically active — they are among the first casualties when the body begins rationing iron for more vital functions.

What to do: Test serum ferritin specifically — not just a standard CBC. If below 70 ng/ml, address with supplementation and dietary changes alongside clinical hair treatment.

02

Thyroid Dysfunction

Hormonal · Often Subclinical

Both hypothyroidism (underactive) and hyperthyroidism (overactive) disrupt the hair growth cycle profoundly. The thyroid gland regulates metabolism at the cellular level — when it malfunctions, follicle cycling slows or becomes erratic. Hair becomes brittle, thin, and begins shedding diffusely across the entire scalp rather than in a pattern.

What makes this deceptive is that subclinical hypothyroidism — where TSH is mildly elevated but T3 and T4 are still technically normal — can cause significant hair loss while blood tests appear almost-normal to a non-specialist eye.

What to do: Test TSH, free T3, and free T4. If thyroid dysfunction is identified, treating the underlying condition is the priority — hair loss typically resolves within 6–12 months of optimal thyroid management.

03

PCOS — Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

Hormonal · Affects 1 in 5 Indian Women

PCOS elevates androgen levels in women — particularly testosterone and its derivative DHT. Just as in androgenetic alopecia in men, elevated DHT miniaturises hair follicles in genetically susceptible women, causing thinning at the crown and frontal hairline. The paradox of PCOS hair loss is that it often presents simultaneously with excess facial or body hair — androgens suppress head hair while stimulating face and body hair growth.

In India, PCOS affects approximately 1 in 5 women of reproductive age and is significantly underdiagnosed — often discovered only when a woman presents with hair loss and the dots are finally connected.

What to do: Hormonal blood panel including testosterone, DHEAS, LH/FSH ratio, alongside a pelvic ultrasound. Treatment combines hormonal management (often oral contraceptives or anti-androgens) with clinical hair therapies like PRP and meso hair therapy.

04

Post-Partum Telogen Effluvium

Hormonal Shift · Temporary But Dramatic

This is the cause that devastates new mothers — often at exactly the moment they least have the emotional reserves to deal with it. During pregnancy, elevated oestrogen extends the anagen growth phase for most follicles. Hair becomes visibly thicker and more lustrous. Then delivery happens. Oestrogen drops sharply. Every follicle that was held in growth phase enters the telogen shedding phase simultaneously.

The result, typically 3–6 months after delivery: dramatic, frightening hair loss that can fill a whole brush in a single stroke. Entire sections of the hairline recede. The scalp becomes visible at the temples and crown. For many mothers, this coincides with post-partum depression, sleep deprivation, and breastfeeding-related nutritional depletion — a compounding pile of stressors on a body already running on empty.

What to do: The reassuring news — this is almost always temporary and self-resolving within 12 months. Address nutritional deficiencies (iron, protein, biotin), manage thyroid function, and consider PRP or meso hair therapy to accelerate recovery and prevent any underlying androgenetic alopecia from taking hold in the meantime.

05

Female Pattern Hair Loss (Androgenetic Alopecia)

Genetic · Progressive Without Treatment

Female pattern hair loss (FPHL) affects an estimated 40% of women by the age of 50 — and is significantly underdiagnosed because it presents differently to male pattern baldness. Rather than a receding hairline, women experience diffuse thinning at the crown and a widening of the central parting, while the frontal hairline is usually preserved. It is often mistaken for normal ageing or dismissed as "just shedding more."

Like its male counterpart, FPHL is driven by genetic sensitivity of hair follicles to DHT — but the hormonal triggers and timeline are different, and the condition is more complex in women because it can be amplified by iron deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, or PCOS running in the background.

What to do: FPHL is progressive without intervention but very manageable with the right treatment. Minoxidil remains first-line, combined with PRP therapy, meso hair therapy, and — where hormone levels permit — anti-androgen medications. Early treatment produces the best outcomes.

06

Nutritional Deficiency — Protein, Biotin & Zinc

Nutritional · Common in Vegetarians

Hair is essentially protein — it is made almost entirely of keratin. A diet chronically low in quality protein impairs the building blocks available for hair shaft production. In India, where vegetarian diets are common and protein-rich foods may be under-consumed, this is a far more frequent contributor to hair loss than most patients or doctors suspect.

Biotin deficiency causes brittle, breaking hair rather than diffuse shedding. Zinc is essential for follicle cell replication — low zinc can impair hair growth and increase shedding. These deficiencies rarely appear in standard blood panels unless specifically requested.

What to do: A detailed dietary history and targeted nutritional panel. Correct deficiencies through diet and supplementation. Note: excessive biotin supplementation from over-the-counter products can actually interfere with certain lab test results — always disclose supplements to your doctor.

07

Scalp Conditions — Seborrheic Dermatitis & Folliculitis

Scalp Health · Treatable

Chronic scalp inflammation from conditions like seborrheic dermatitis (a common fungal-triggered scalp condition causing dandruff, itching, and flaking) or folliculitis (infected follicles) directly impairs hair growth by creating an inflammatory environment around the follicle. Untreated, persistent scalp inflammation can contribute to gradual follicle miniaturisation over time.

This is a cause that many women never consider because they are focused on the hair rather than the scalp beneath it. A scalp that is perpetually inflamed, itchy, or flaking is a scalp that cannot optimally support hair growth — regardless of what else you do.

What to do: Trichoscopy assessment of the scalp to identify the condition accurately, followed by targeted prescription antifungal or anti-inflammatory treatments. Clinical scalp detox sessions at Aesthetica help reset the scalp microbiome and create the environment hair needs to grow.

And Now — About Stress

Stress does belong on this list — but at the end, not the beginning, and always in context.

Extreme physiological stress — surgery, serious illness, a period of severe under-eating, or a major acute emotional trauma — can trigger telogen effluvium: a diffuse shedding that happens 2–4 months after the stressor. This is temporary and self-resolving.

What chronic, low-grade workplace or life stress cannot do is cause the kind of progressive, patterned, accelerating hair loss that brings most women to my clinic. If stress alone were to blame, every person under deadline pressure in Pune would be visibly thinning. Most of them are not.

The most important thing I can tell you: Female hair loss almost always has a diagnosable, treatable cause. The earlier you investigate, the more follicles are still in the recoverable window. Don't wait until you can see scalp through hair to take it seriously. Come in when you first notice the drain clogging more than it should.

The Woman With the Ziploc Bag

Her blood work told the story clearly: ferritin at 24 ng/ml (severely depleted), mildly elevated TSH, and a trichoscopy that revealed early-stage androgenetic alopecia running underneath everything else. Three things. Not one. Not stress.

We corrected the iron deficiency aggressively, referred her endocrinologist for thyroid optimisation, started a course of PRP alongside meso hair therapy, and added prescription minoxidil. She came back at month four with a very different energy in the room. Not just because the hair was recovering — though it was, measurably — but because she finally had an answer.

That Ziploc bag was the beginning of a diagnosis, not a death sentence. Female hair loss almost always is.
